# Law of Torts in India

## Definition
A tort is a civil wrong (other than breach of contract) for which the remedy is a common law action for unliquidated damages. Derived from Roman law "tortum" (twisted/wrong).

## Essential Elements of a Tort
1. **Wrongful Act or Omission**: Act must be legally wrongful — either violating a legal right or committing a legal wrong.
2. **Legal Damage**: Actual damage (damnum) or violation of a legal right (injuria).
   - **Injuria sine damno**: Legal right violated without actual damage — still actionable (Ashby v. White, 1703 — right to vote).
   - **Damnum sine injuria**: Actual damage without violation of legal right — not actionable (Gloucester Grammar School Case — competition causing financial loss).
3. **Legal Remedy**: Damages (compensation).

## General Defences

### Volenti Non Fit Injuria (Consent)
"To one who is willing, no injury is done." Plaintiff voluntarily assumed the risk.
- Must be free and voluntary consent.
- Consent to the act, not merely to the risk.
- Does not apply if the defendant was negligent beyond the assumed risk.

### Plaintiff's Own Negligence (Contributory Negligence)
- If plaintiff's own negligence contributed to the harm, damages are reduced proportionally.
- Last Opportunity Rule: Person who had the last opportunity to avoid harm is liable.

### Act of God (Vis Major)
Natural event of extraordinary nature that could not be reasonably anticipated or guarded against.
Examples: Flood, earthquake, lightning.

### Inevitable Accident
An accident that was not avoidable by any such precautions as a reasonable person could be expected to take.

### Private Defence
Acts done in reasonable defence of person or property.

### Statutory Authority
Acts authorised by statute cannot be tortious even if they cause damage.
- Must be strictly within the authority granted.
- Negligence in exercise of statutory authority is not protected.

### Necessity
Interference with plaintiff's rights to prevent greater harm.
- **Public Necessity**: Destroying property to prevent spread of fire (complete defence).
- **Private Necessity**: Incomplete defence; liability for actual damage may remain.

## Negligence

### Elements
1. **Duty of Care**: Defendant owed plaintiff a legal duty of care.
2. **Breach**: Defendant breached that duty by falling below the standard of a reasonable person.
3. **Causation**: Breach caused the harm (factual causation — "but for" test; legal causation — remoteness).
4. **Damage**: Plaintiff suffered actual damage.

### Neighbour Principle (Donoghue v. Stevenson, 1932)
You owe a duty of care to persons so closely and directly affected by your acts that you ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.

### Standard of Care
Reasonable person (objective standard). Professionals: Reasonable person with that professional's skill (Bolam test for medical negligence).

### Res Ipsa Loquitur
"The thing speaks for itself." Negligence inferred from the circumstances without direct proof.
Conditions: (1) Defendant in control of the thing, (2) accident of a kind that would not normally occur without negligence, (3) defendant has not explained away the negligence.

### Remoteness of Damage
Defendant liable only for damage that was reasonably foreseeable (**Wagon Mound test**, 1961).

## Strict and Absolute Liability

### Rule in Rylands v. Fletcher (1868) — Strict Liability
A person who brings onto their land something likely to do mischief if it escapes is liable for all direct damage caused by the escape, even without negligence.

**Conditions**: Non-natural use of land + escape + damage.

**Defences**: Act of God, plaintiff's own fault, act of stranger, statutory authority, plaintiff's consent.

### Absolute Liability (M.C. Mehta v. Union of India, 1987)
Indian Supreme Court evolved a stricter rule: Enterprises engaged in hazardous activities are absolutely liable for any harm caused, with NO exceptions.
- Compensation proportional to magnitude and capacity of enterprise.
- Applies to industries like chemical plants, gas factories, nuclear plants.

## Defamation

### Definition
Publication of a false statement of fact that lowers a person in the estimation of right-thinking members of society.

### Types
- **Libel**: Written/permanent form — actionable per se (without proof of damage).
- **Slander**: Spoken/transient form — requires proof of damage in most cases.

### Elements
1. Defamatory statement.
2. Reference to the plaintiff.
3. Publication to a third party.

### Defences
- **Truth (Justification)**: Complete defence — truth is an absolute defence in civil defamation.
- **Fair Comment**: Honest opinion on a matter of public interest.
- **Privilege**:
  - Absolute Privilege: Parliamentary proceedings, judicial proceedings, state communications.
  - Qualified Privilege: Communications made in performance of legal/moral/social duty.

### Criminal Defamation (Section 499/500 IPC)
Punishable up to 2 years + fine. Making or publishing imputation intending to harm reputation.

## Nuisance

### Private Nuisance
Unlawful interference with a person's use and enjoyment of their land.
- Must be substantial and unreasonable.
- Factors: Locality, duration, sensitivity of plaintiff.

### Public Nuisance
Interference with the rights of the public at large.
- Prosecuted as a crime.
- Civil action available to individual who suffers special damage above and beyond the public.

## Vicarious Liability
One person held liable for the torts of another due to a special relationship.

### Master and Servant (Employer-Employee)
Employer liable for torts committed by employee in the **course of employment**.
- Authorised acts done in an unauthorised way.
- Frolic: If employee departs entirely from their employment purpose, employer not liable.

### Principal and Agent
Principal liable for authorised acts of agent and acts within apparent authority.

### Partners
Each partner liable for torts of other partners committed in the course of partnership business.

## Trespass

### Trespass to Person
- **Assault**: Apprehension of immediate harmful/offensive contact.
- **Battery**: Direct and intentional harmful/offensive contact with person.
- **False Imprisonment**: Complete restraint of freedom of movement without lawful justification.

### Trespass to Land
Intentional, direct entry onto land in possession of another without consent.

### Trespass to Goods
Intentional interference with goods in possession of another.

## Product Liability in Tort
Manufacturer, distributor, or seller may be liable for defective products causing harm.
- **Negligence**: Failure to exercise reasonable care in design, manufacture, warning.
- **Strict Liability**: For inherently dangerous products (absolute liability in India).

## Remedies in Tort
1. **Damages**: Compensatory (special + general), nominal, contemptuous, exemplary/punitive.
2. **Injunction**: Restraining order to stop continuing tort.
3. **Self-help**: Abatement of nuisance, re-entry on land, recaption of goods (limited circumstances).
4. **Restitution of property**.

## Key Case Laws
- **Donoghue v. Stevenson (1932)**: Neighbour principle; duty of care in negligence.
- **Rylands v. Fletcher (1868)**: Strict liability for escape of dangerous things.
- **M.C. Mehta v. Union of India (1987)**: Absolute liability for hazardous industries in India.
- **Bhim Singh v. State of J&K (1985)**: False imprisonment; compensation for illegal detention.
- **Jacob Mathew v. State of Punjab (2005)**: Medical negligence — Bolam test applied in India.
- **Bhopal Gas Tragedy Settlement (1989)**: ₹470 crore settlement for mass tort — led to development of absolute liability principle.
