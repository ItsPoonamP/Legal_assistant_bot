# Force Majeure Clause in Indian Law

## Definition
Force majeure (French: "superior force") is a contractual clause that excuses a party from performing its obligations when extraordinary events beyond its control make performance impossible, impractical, or illegal.

## Legal Basis in India

### Section 56 — Doctrine of Frustration
"An agreement to do an act impossible in itself is void. A contract to do an act which, after the contract is made, becomes impossible, or by reason of some event which the promisor could not prevent, unlawful, becomes void when the act becomes impossible or unlawful."

**Key distinction**: Force majeure is a **contractual** remedy; Section 56 is a **statutory** remedy. If a contract has an express force majeure clause, it governs. If not, Section 56 applies.

## What Qualifies as Force Majeure

### Typically Included Events
- Natural disasters: earthquakes, floods, cyclones, tsunamis
- War, armed conflict, invasion, terrorism
- Government actions: sanctions, embargoes, change in law
- Epidemics, pandemics (COVID-19 recognized in many contracts)
- Strikes, labour disputes (sometimes excluded)
- Fire, explosion
- Power failures, infrastructure collapse

### Typically Excluded Events
- Economic hardship or financial difficulty
- Market fluctuations, price increases
- Supply chain difficulties (if foreseeable)
- Events foreseeable at time of contracting
- Events within the party's control

## COVID-19 and Force Majeure in India
The Ministry of Finance (Office Memorandum, February 19, 2020) clarified that COVID-19 is a natural calamity and may be considered a force majeure event for government contracts. However, courts have scrutinized each claim individually.

**Key judicial positions:**
- Mere difficulty in performance is not force majeure.
- Financial inability to perform does not invoke force majeure.
- The event must directly cause the inability to perform.

## Essential Requirements to Invoke Force Majeure

1. **Existence**: The clause must be expressly included in the contract.
2. **Covered Event**: The event must fall within the defined list.
3. **Causation**: The event must have directly caused the inability to perform.
4. **Notification**: Most clauses require prompt written notice (typically 7–30 days).
5. **Mitigation**: The affected party must take reasonable steps to overcome the obstacle.
6. **Beyond Control**: The event must be beyond the party's reasonable control.

## Effect of Force Majeure

- **Suspension**: Obligations are suspended for the duration of the event.
- **Termination**: If the event continues beyond a specified period, either party may terminate.
- **Partial Performance**: If only part of the obligation is affected, the rest must be performed.
- **No Damages**: Neither party is liable for delay or non-performance caused by the event.

## Standard Force Majeure Clause (Template)
"Neither party shall be liable for any failure or delay in performance of its obligations under this Agreement to the extent such failure or delay is caused by circumstances beyond its reasonable control, including but not limited to acts of God, natural disasters, war, terrorism, riots, embargoes, acts of civil or military authorities, fire, floods, accidents, strikes, or shortages of transportation, facilities, fuel, energy, labour, or materials ("Force Majeure Event"), provided that:
(a) the affected party gives prompt written notice to the other party;
(b) the affected party uses reasonable efforts to overcome or work around the Force Majeure Event;
(c) the suspension of performance is of no greater scope and no longer duration than required by the Force Majeure Event."

## Distinction: Force Majeure vs. Frustration vs. Hardship

| Aspect | Force Majeure | Frustration (S.56) | Hardship |
|---|---|---|---|
| Source | Contractual clause | Statutory doctrine | Contractual clause |
| Trigger | Specified events | Impossibility/illegality | Fundamental change in circumstances |
| Effect | Suspension/termination | Contract void automatically | Renegotiation |
| Proof | Event within clause + causation | Radical change in obligation | Excessive onerousness |

## Key Case Laws
- **Energy Watchdog v. CERC (2017)**: Supreme Court held that a rise in fuel prices is not a force majeure event; parties must accept commercial risks they undertook.
- **Satyabrata Ghose v. Mugneeram Bangur (1954)**: Section 56 applies when performance becomes impossible; frustration extinguishes the contract.
- **Standard Retail Pvt. Ltd. v. G.S. Global Corp (2020)**: Bombay High Court rejected COVID-19 as force majeure for steel import contracts as there was no direct causation.
- **M/s Halliburton Offshore Services Inc. v. Vedanta Ltd. (2020)**: Delhi High Court recognized COVID-19 lockdown as a force majeure event affecting construction timelines.
